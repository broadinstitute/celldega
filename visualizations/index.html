    Visualizations

    Celldega's interactive views help you explore where cells are, how gene expression varies, and which biological processes distinguish a group. Use linked views to move between tissue context, expression patterns, and gene-set interpretation.

    For desktop viewing, try Celldega-App, with linked Landscape–Clustergram panels, Yearbook cell portraits, and saved Clustergrams. Download the latest build or see the desktop guide and screenshots.

    Video tutorial

    This narrated walkthrough links a Landscape, Clustergram, and Enrich to explore a Visium HD human colorectal cancer dataset. Follow along in the Visium HD Human CRC Landscape-Clustergram-Enrich notebook.

    Spatial visualizations

    Explore cells and neighborhoods in physical space (x/y, and sometimes z) across a tissue section, or compare cells in a low dimensional embedding landscape (UMAP/XY).

    • Landscape: Where are cell types in the tissue, and which cells express a gene? Explore cells, transcripts, images, and neighborhoods over tissue, or switch to UMAP and gene-expression XY views.
    • Yearbook: What do individual cells look like? Compare a grid of cell portraits, each centered on one cell from the same spatial dataset, and mark cells for review.
    • CellCloud: How are cells arranged in 3D? Explore thick tissue and aligned serial sections.
    • NeighborhoodCloud: How do tissue neighborhoods extend across slices? Explore neighborhoods and their cells in 3D.

    Data visualizations

    Compare measured values across cells, clusters, neighborhoods, or samples.

    • Clustergram: Which genes distinguish each cell group, and which groups are most alike? Find expression patterns in a clustered heatmap or dot plot, with dendrograms and gene/metadata labels.
    • Composition: How do cell-type proportions differ across samples, regions, or neighborhoods?

    Information visualization

    Interpret the genes that distinguish a selected group.

    • Enrich: Which biological processes or cell types are associated with a set of genes? Explore gene-set enrichment against public libraries through Enrichr.

    Linked Visualization

    Connect views of the same dataset to explore cell positions, expression patterns, and gene-set enrichment together.

    • Landscape–Clustergram–Enrich: connect tissue or embedding views with expression patterns and gene-set interpretation.
    • CellCloud–Clustergram: connect cells in 3D with expression patterns across cell groups.

    Start with the Gallery for interactive examples or the Example Notebooks for complete workflows. Each visualization's guide explains what it shows and how to create it; the Python API reference covers constructor options and notebook controls.

    The control panel

    Landscape, Yearbook, CellCloud, and NeighborhoodCloud share a control panel above the view. Clustergram, Composition, and Enrich have their own panels, described on their pages.

    The Landscape control panel over a whole-transcriptome kidney section, showing glomeruli and tubules. Cells are colored by cluster over the DAPI, boundary, and RNA image channels.

    Control What it does
    1 View and image The view menu switches between tissue (SPATIAL), embedding (UMAP, when the data include one), and two-gene (XY) views: it shows the current view, and its ▾ lists the others. IMG shows or hides the image; each channel button toggles that channel, and its slider sets the channel's brightness. SNAP saves a Snapshot with a memo, and PINS shows pinned Snapshots on the tissue (it appears when there are pins).
    2 CELL Shows or hides the cells; the slider sets their size. The bars rank cell clusters by count: click a bar to select that cluster, and shift-click to add more.
    3 TRX Transcripts and gene expression. Zoomed in, TRX shows or hides individual transcripts and the slider sets their size; zoomed out, the slider sets how strongly cells are colored by a selected gene. The bars rank genes: click one to color cells by its expression.
    4 NBHD Neighborhoods: shows supplied tissue regions, or lets you draw your own. Appears only when neighborhoods are supplied or drawing is turned on.
    5 Gene search Type one or more gene names, separated by commas, to color cells by expression; clear the box to return to cluster colors. The box below describes the selected gene, from UniProt.
    6 Celldega logo Opens this visualization's documentation page.
    7 The view Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, hover over cells and transcripts for details, and click a cell to select its cluster.

    Conventions shared across visualizations:

    • Blue button text means on; gray means off. A faded button is not available at the current zoom.
    • Scrolling over a Landscape, CellCloud, or Clustergram zooms it instead of scrolling the page. Move the pointer off the visualization to scroll the page.
    • Hovering shows details in a tooltip, without changing the selection; clicking selects. For genes, hovering shows a brief UniProt description and clicking shows it in the gene information box.
    • The Celldega logo in every control panel links to that visualization's page in this documentation.

    Two ways to use a visualization

    Each visualization, such as a Landscape or a Clustergram, runs in your web browser. You can use it in two ways:

     Notebook widget (Jupyter or marimo)      JavaScript on a web page
┌────────────────────────────────────┐   ┌────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Python kernel                      │   │ Gallery, standalone web page,      │
│   AnnData, Matrix, your analysis   │   │ or notebook report (no Python)     │
│     │ options        ▲ selections, │   │                                    │
│     ▼ and data       │ drawings    │   │                                    │
│ Browser                            │   │ Browser                            │
│   Landscape ◄─link─► Clustergram   │   │   Landscape ◄─link─► Clustergram   │
└────────────────────────────────────┘   └────────────────────────────────────┘
                   ▲                                        ▲
                   └───────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                                       │
           prepared data (DegaFiles or AnnData Zarr), read over HTTP
    • As a notebook widget with a Python back-end. Create the visualization from your analysis in Jupyter or marimo. The Python back-end makes the widget stateful: selections, drawn neighborhoods, and annotations are available to your analysis, and Python can update the view. Links between widgets, such as a Clustergram click coloring a Landscape, run in the browser through the widget framework (jslink).

    You can also embed visualizations in notebook reports: static HTML pages made from pre-executed notebooks saved with their widget state. The visualizations and their links stay interactive in the report, without a running kernel. The Example Notebooks on this site are shown this way. - As JavaScript on a web page, without Python. The Gallery pages, standalone pages built with the JavaScript API, and notebook reports all run the same visualizations in the browser. Every interaction and browser link works; there is no Python back-end to receive selections or annotations.

    Each visualization's page describes its interactions, which are the same in both cases, and then what the widget's Python back-end adds.

    Reading and driving visualizations from code

    Every visualization can be read and changed from code as well as by clicking: getters report what it shows, setters change it as its controls would, and exporters capture it as an image. The same keys work in Python (widget.request_state(), widget.apply_state(), widget.snapshot()) and JavaScript; see the Viz API.

    Snapshots

    Snapshot is Celldega's name for a captured visualization that combines three parts:

    • Screenshot: a raster image of what the visualization showed at capture time.
    • Memo: a description of what you noticed and why it matters, supplied through description.
    • Reproducibility metadata: the data source and identity, supported view settings, observed state, author, capture time and renderer version needed to interpret the observation and revisit its view.

    The screenshot is the visual component of a Snapshot. The complete artifact keeps that image together with its explanation and context. Like Landscape, Snapshot names a specific Celldega concept. Its goal is to preserve an interactive observation as reproducible visual evidence.

    Think of a Snapshot as a postcard: a picture of a place in your data, with an explanation of why it matters and a way to return. The in-memory capture and its PNG export are both Snapshots. Python saves a PNG and a human-readable Markdown companion:

    • <name>.png: the image, with your description, who made it and when, the Celldega version, each widget's saved view (snapshot, with its replay settings in snapshot.state), its observed state (observed_state), what was in view (cell counts per cluster, top genes), and the data it came from (dataset name, base URL or AnnData Zarr URL, AnnData shape, the Matrix's source) embedded in its metadata;
    • <name>.md: the description, the image, provenance and code to reopen the view.

    The PNG alone carries the saved view; the Markdown companion is for readers and is not needed for replay.

    Why Snapshots: annotating what you find

    Celldega already lets you annotate a dataset's molecular side: label clusters with cell types, group genes, and draw neighborhoods around regions of tissue. Much of what you notice while exploring is a particular view, though: a ring of cells around a structure, two genes that meet at a boundary, an odd patch at one zoom level. A drawn region doesn't capture that well, and an ordinary screenshot loses it: the image keeps no record of the dataset, the settings, or how to get back to it.

    Snapshots are annotations of that kind. Each one keeps the view's spatial evidence, your explanation, and the settings to reopen it, inside the PNG itself, so the context travels with the image. You can take them yourself with SNAP, and Clerk takes them while it explores. Put together, they make something you can share instead of a narrated screen recording:

    • a notebook of free text and Snapshot cards, each card linked to a live visualization so clicking it opens that view;
    • a Landscape with pins marking points of interest, each pin opening the Snapshot behind it.

    The spatial evidence then sits beside the molecular annotations, such as cell type labels and marker genes, in a form a collaborator can open and check.

    The idea isn't limited to tissue. Pins also go on a UMAP, so a single-cell dataset (an AnnData Zarr in a Landscape) can be an annotated embedding. Snapshots of a Clustergram or a Yearbook have no map to pin to, but make the same cards: a heatmap view, or a page of cells, with your notes and a link back.

    The saved view records what data it shows (cell, gene and cluster names, or a Clustergram's row and column names). Image and state are captured together. widget.restore_snapshot(...) checks the schema, data identity and canvas size before changing the view; see Portable Snapshots for reopening exports and declaring an exact data revision.

    Records are marked restoration: "partial". They preserve the image and provenance, and from_png can load a recorded source into a fresh widget, but not every control is restored. See replay coverage and limitations.

    Save one from a widget's control panel with SNAP: it captures the view, then asks for a name and a memo of what you're looking at and why it's interesting. Or from Python, on a Landscape, Clustergram, Yearbook, CellCloud or NeighborhoodCloud. In a web page without a notebook kernel, SNAP downloads a PNG with the replay state and description embedded:

    landscape.snapshot(
    name="Glomerulus",
    description="Podocyte-like cells ring each glomerulus; the ring breaks where ...",
    path="figures/",            # a folder, or a file like "figures/fig2a.png"
)
# A linked row (or several widgets) becomes one image, side by side:
dega.viz.snapshot(linked, description="...", path="figures/fig2.png")

    The image arrives from the browser after the cell finishes, so the call's output shows "Capturing…" and then a preview with the saved path. Without a path, Snapshots go to celldega_files/snapshots/ (or widget.snapshot_dir). This export directory can be kept out of version control or shared as a collection of evidence. Areas the visualization leaves empty are white, so the image looks the same in light and dark viewers; pass background="black" (any CSS color) or background=None for transparency.

    The PNG records links, not data: a widget's base_url or zarr_url, and a Matrix's Zarr source when it was opened with Matrix.read_zarr, plus constructor options. An object passed in memory, such as adata=adata or a Matrix built in the notebook, has no path to record (read_h5ad keeps none). The PNG stores its shape and marks it as needed again when reopening. The whole record is typically a few KB.

    Record what Python can't see, such as the AnnData file a widget was made from, once per widget; it's added to every Snapshot of it, including SNAP ones. This is a note for readers: reopening still needs the object passed back.

    landscape.snapshot_metadata = {"adata": "data/kidney.h5ad", "notebook": "review.ipynb"}

    Reopen the image as an interactive visualization:

    view = dega.viz.from_png("figures/fig2a.png")
display(view)
# In a later cell: None while pending, else applied/apply_error.
view.get_apply_result(view.apply_request_id)

    The original data must still be available. Supply matrix=mat or adata=adata when the original view used an in-memory object; constructor options can point to a relocated source. For a composite Snapshot, panel=1 opens the second widget. Use widget.restore_png(path) to restore into a widget that already has its data. See Portable Snapshots for browser usage, validation and restoration limits.

    Inspect the embedded record with dega.viz.load_snapshot(path) and discover a folder's PNG records with dega.viz.load_snapshots(folder). A folder of Snapshots works as a scrapbook of what you found along the way, reusable by people and application agents: Clerk can read it as a briefing or add its own.

    Postcards in a notebook

    Saving with SNAP or widget.snapshot(name="...") also adds the capture to the viewer's snap dictionary. Names are preserved exactly; repeated names receive (2), (3), etc. Use list(landscape.snap) to see them. The collection is local to that Python instance; the PNG and Markdown files persist on disk.

    After the capture finishes, display a postcard in a later notebook cell:

    from celldega.viz import Snapshot

Snapshot(landscape=landscape, snapshot="Glomerulus")

    The card shows a static PNG, its name and memo. Snapshot metadata expands the provenance and replay settings. The blue Show this view link restores the linked viewer and reports any failure. Several cards can point to the same viewer; place them between Markdown cells to explain an analysis step by step.

    # A file is also usable after restarting the kernel; link the newly displayed viewer.
Snapshot("figures/glomerulus.png", landscape=landscape)
Snapshot(clustergram=cgm, snapshot="Markers")
Snapshot(yearbook=yb, snapshot="Interesting cells")

# An unlinked postcard, including an ordinary PNG with your own caption:
Snapshot("figures/overview.png", name="Overview", description="Start here.")
# Or the in-memory image and state from request_snapshot():
Snapshot(landscape.latest_snapshot, landscape=landscape)

    Cards embed their PNG and text in notebook widget state and do not load the dataset or visualization renderer. Save notebook widget state to keep them when sharing a notebook. Navigation needs a live kernel and the linked viewer displayed. Ordinary PNGs have no replay metadata, so they show no navigation link. For a composite PNG, panel=1 chooses the second viewer's state; the card still displays the composite.

    Navigation accepts a different viewer size by default (allow_resize=False requires the original size), but always checks data identity. Yearbook Snapshots preserve the actual ordered cells, page, zoom, checked cells, colors and supported layer controls; the grid layout must match. The original data must remain available.

    Pins: points of interest

    Snapshots can also annotate the tissue itself, like pins on a map of places you've been, each with a photo and a postcard about it. Give a Landscape a collection of Snapshots and each one becomes a pin at the center of its view. PINS shows or hides them; hovering a pin shows its Snapshot's name and memo, and clicking it opens its view. Where drawn neighborhoods outline regions, pins mark points of interest with whatever explanation you write, for your future self or a collaborator.

    poi = dega.viz.Snapshots(landscape.snap)          # the Snapshots you saved with SNAP
poi = dega.viz.Snapshots("celldega_files/snapshots")  # or every Snapshot PNG in a folder
poi = dega.viz.Snapshots(["glomerulus.png", "medulla.png"])  # or chosen files

landscape.pins = poi
landscape.show_pins = True  # or press PINS

# Pins can also be pre-loaded when the Landscape is created:
landscape = dega.viz.Landscape(base_url=..., snapshots=poi, show_pins=True)

    Snapshots is a small named collection (poi.names(), poi["Glomerulus"], poi.add(path, name=...), and poi.card("Glomerulus", landscape=landscape) for a postcard chosen by name). While shown, the pins are also listed by name in place of the image controls; click one to open its view. Snapshots of other data than the Landscape's are skipped, with a warning. A pin sits in the space its Snapshot shows, the tissue or the UMAP, and appears while the Landscape shows that space; the list names them all and marks those in the other space, which opening switches to. XY Snapshots aren't pinned (their axes depend on the genes chosen), and Clustergram and Yearbook Snapshots are shown as cards instead. Pins live in the widget's state, so opening one works in a saved notebook or static page without a kernel. In JavaScript, pass pins (or pin_data) and show_pins to celldega.landscape(el, {...}): each pin is {name, description, target: [x, y], space, snapshot}, with space "spatial" (the default) or "umap".

    The former screenshot(), load_screenshot(), load_screenshots(), screenshot_dir and screenshot_metadata spellings remain compatibility aliases.
